# Case Study (Mid-Term) — Instructor Materials

**Canvas assignment:** Case Study (Mid-Term) · 100 pts · Case Study group (15% of course grade) · Due 10/2/26
**Competency:** Comp 7 (Assess) — formally assessed here, per `modules/module-04.md`
**Draws on:** Modules 01–04 only (mandated reporting, the multisystem response, neglect vs. poverty-related hardship, physical abuse, disproportionality) — nothing from Module 05+ (ICWA, trafficking, immigration) should be expected knowledge yet.

Both case families below are **entirely fictional composites** — no real person, family, or case file. Family composition, referral pattern, and the racial breakdown across the two cases are grounded in actual Georgia child welfare data (cited below) rather than picked arbitrarily, so the demographic choices are defensible in a classroom setting rather than reflecting an instructor's assumption.

Both cases are now explicitly set in **Fulton County** and **DeKalb County** — the two counties actually covered by the *Kenny A. v. Perdue* consent decree (introduced in Module 02, revisited in Module 06). That's not incidental: it means the Foster Care Services and Case Disposition phases can require students to reason with the decree's real, named practice standards rather than generic "best practice" placeholders.

## Data grounding

- Neglect accounts for **64%** of substantiated maltreatment cases in Georgia — the majority type, well ahead of physical abuse. ([PCA Georgia](https://pcaga.gsu.edu/child-abuse-neglect-statistics-in-georgia/))
- In 2023, Georgia received reports on roughly 100,000 children; **11,435** had a confirmed (substantiated) case. ([PCA Georgia](https://pcaga.gsu.edu/child-abuse-neglect-statistics-in-georgia/))
- Racial breakdown of substantiated child victims in Georgia: **Black/African American 41.9%, White 41.2%, Hispanic 8%, Multiple Race 6.4%.** ([CWLA Georgia Fact Sheet 2025](https://www.cwla.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Georgia-2025.pdf))
- Black residents are roughly a third of Georgia's total population ([Black Demographics — Georgia](https://blackdemographics.com/states/georgia-black-population/)) — meaning Black children are substantiated as maltreatment victims at a rate visibly higher than their share of the population. That gap is the empirical basis for building disproportionality into Case A, and it's real, current, and citable — not an instructor assumption. (Assign students to pull the latest KIDS COUNT child-population-by-race figure for Georgia themselves as part of the case; the exact child-only percentage shifts year to year and finding it is itself a small data-literacy exercise.)
- Nationally, Black children have the highest confirmed-maltreatment victimization rate (11.8 per 1,000) of any racial group tracked by NCANDS. ([Child Welfare Monitor](https://childwelfaremonitor.org/2023/04/30/what-is-the-cause-of-racial-disparity-in-child-welfare/))
- *Kenny A. v. Perdue*: filed 2002 by Children's Rights on behalf of children in foster care in **Fulton and DeKalb Counties**; the 2005 consent decree requires the state to achieve and sustain 31 outcome measures, including caseworker caseload limits, **sibling co-placement** (siblings entering placement together must be placed together unless harmful, one has needs requiring a specialized program, or group size makes it impractical despite diligent efforts), timely health screenings for children entering care, and reduced reliance on congregate/group care — monitored by independent accountability agents issuing public reports every six months. ([Georgia DFCS](https://dfcs.georgia.gov/data/federal-regulations-and-data/kenny-vs-sonny-perdue-consent-decree)) Exit from monitoring requires three consecutive compliant six-month reporting periods — check the DFCS page for current status before teaching this, since it changes.

Consistent with what Module 04 already teaches (disproportionality ≠ proof of differential *conduct* — it can reflect differential *surveillance and reporting thresholds* across the same conduct), Case A is written so the facts are genuinely ambiguous between "neglect" and "poverty," and the assignment prompts require students to argue both readings before landing on a recommendation.

## Case Study A — Neglect (with disproportionality)

**Family:** The Whitfield family — Denise Whitfield (34, Black, single mother) and her three children: Marcus (9), Aaliyah (6), and Josiah (2). Denise works two part-time jobs (home health aide, evening shifts at a grocery store) and does not have a car; she relies on a patchwork of a neighbor, a cousin, and the city bus to cover childcare gaps. The family lives in a two-bedroom apartment in **Fulton County, Georgia** — squarely inside the *Kenny A.* consent decree's jurisdiction, which matters directly in Phase 3.

**Referral:** A report comes from Marcus's elementary school counselor (school personnel are the most common referral source in Georgia, consistent with statewide reporting patterns). Marcus has been arriving late and occasionally unwashed twice in the past month; on one occasion Aaliyah was dropped at school by an unfamiliar teenage cousin. The counselor also notes the family "seems to be struggling" and that Josiah has missed two pediatric well-checks according to a note from the family's Medicaid care coordinator.

**Deliberately ambiguous facts** (for students to weigh, not resolve for them):
- No allegation of physical harm, and no marks/injuries noted by anyone.
- Denise has never missed a rent payment and the apartment, per the reporting counselor's secondhand description, is "cluttered but not unsafe."
- The missed well-checks track closely with a stretch when Denise's second job's hours changed with no advance notice.
- Denise has not been contacted directly by anyone yet at the point the report is filed.

### Phase 1: Intake
Prompts for students:
- Applying the reasonable-cause standard from Module 01, does this report meet the threshold to screen in? Argue it both ways before you commit.
- Who else in the multisystem network (Module 02) should realistically be looped in here — school, Medicaid/health, transportation/community resources — and in what order?
- **Disproportionality prompt:** Georgia's substantiated child-victim population is roughly 42% Black against a Black population share of roughly a third statewide (cite the current KIDS COUNT figure you find). If the Whitfield family were white and otherwise identical, would you expect the same referral to have been made by the same reporter? What does your answer imply about where in this case the intervention should actually focus — the family, or the systems (transportation, job-schedule volatility, healthcare access) around it?

### Phase 2: Ongoing/In-Home Services
Prompts:
- Build a case plan that responds to the *actual* barriers in the fact pattern (transportation, job-schedule volatility, healthcare access) rather than a generic "parenting classes" plan.
- Describe an engagement approach consistent with Module 04's multidimensional engagement content — specifically, how would you build trust with a mother who may reasonably perceive this investigation as targeting her poverty rather than her parenting?

### Phase 3: Foster Care Services
Prompts:
- Does this fact pattern, on its own, support removal? If in-home services fail or a new safety concern emerges, who among Denise's existing support network (the neighbor, the cousin) should be assessed first for kinship placement, and what would need to be true for that to be appropriate?
- If no kin placement is viable, what should a caseworker weigh in deciding whether foster placement is warranted at all versus intensifying in-home services?
- **Kenny A. application:** if Marcus, Aaliyah, and Josiah were removed, the consent decree's sibling co-placement standard would presume they're placed together — unless doing so would be harmful to one of them, one has needs a specialized program alone can meet, or the group's size makes it genuinely impractical despite diligent efforts. Does anything in this fact pattern come close to those exceptions, or is a three-sibling placement realistically achievable in Fulton County? What would a caseworker need to document to justify splitting them up if it came to that?
- Given Josiah's missed well-checks, what would the consent decree's timely-health-screening standard require of the agency if he entered care — and does the fact pattern suggest that standard would actually be met?

### Phase 4: Case Disposition
Prompts:
- Write a disposition recommendation (case closure, continued in-home services, or removal) with a rationale that explicitly traces back to Phases 1–3.
- Name one piece of evidence that would change your recommendation if it turned out to be true (e.g., "if Josiah's missed well-checks were actually about lack of transportation, my recommendation would be X; if it turned out Denise had been offered transportation assistance and declined repeatedly, my recommendation would be Y").
- If your recommendation involves any form of placement outside the home, state explicitly whether it satisfies the *Kenny A.* sibling co-placement standard and how you'd verify that.

## Case Study B — Physical Abuse

**Family:** The Renner family — Kyle Renner (29, White) and Ashley Renner (27, White), married, and their son Tyler (5). Kyle recently lost his job and has been the primary daytime caregiver for Tyler while Ashley works full-time. The household has a documented history of a domestic disturbance call (police responded, no arrest made) eight months prior to this referral. The family lives in **DeKalb County, Georgia** — the second county covered by *Kenny A.*, alongside Fulton.

**Referral:** Tyler's pediatrician, during a same-day sick visit for an ear infection, notices bruising on Tyler's upper arm consistent with a grip pattern and a healing mark on his lower back. Tyler tells the nurse, unprompted, that "Daddy gets really mad now that he's home all the time." The pediatrician's office files the report (medical personnel are among the most common mandated-reporter sources nationally, alongside education).

### Phase 1: Intake
Prompts:
- Applying the reasonable-cause standard, does the combination of physical findings + a child's spontaneous statement meet the threshold to screen in? What's the minimum additional information a caseworker would want before the first home visit?
- The prior domestic disturbance call didn't result in an investigation at the time. Should it have? What does its resurfacing now tell you about how prior non-substantiated contacts with a family should (or shouldn't) be weighted later?
- Who in the multisystem network should be contacted first here, and does the order differ from Case A's — why or why not?

### Phase 2: Ongoing/In-Home Services
Prompts:
- Kyle's job loss and the shift in caregiving roles are plausible stressors, not excuses. Build a case plan that addresses both child safety and the underlying stressor, and explain how you'd sequence them.
- How does engaging Ashley (the non-offending parent, if that's what the assessment finds) differ from engaging Kyle? What are the risks of treating Ashley as simply a bystander?

### Phase 3: Foster Care Services
Prompts:
- Under what specific conditions would this case escalate to removal versus a safety plan that keeps Tyler in the home (e.g., Kyle temporarily out of the home, supervised contact)?
- If removal is warranted, is a family member on Ashley's or Kyle's side automatically a safer kinship option than a foster placement — what would a caseworker need to verify before assuming so?
- **Kenny A. application:** Tyler is an only child, so sibling co-placement isn't at issue here — but the consent decree's push to reduce reliance on group/congregate care is. If no kinship placement is available, what does that standard imply about the type of foster placement a caseworker should pursue for a 5-year-old, and why would a group facility be a poor fit even setting the consent decree aside?
- The consent decree also sets caseworker caseload limits. Why does a caseload standard matter concretely for a case like Tyler's, where ongoing monitoring of a safety plan (not just a single decision point) is central to keeping him safely at home?

### Phase 4: Case Disposition
Prompts:
- Write a disposition recommendation with rationale tracing back to Phases 1–3.
- Compare, in a few sentences, how the disposition process for Case B differed from Case A — same four phases, but what changed about how evidence was weighted, and which *Kenny A.* standard ended up mattering most for each case (sibling co-placement for Case A vs. congregate-care avoidance/caseload limits for Case B)?
