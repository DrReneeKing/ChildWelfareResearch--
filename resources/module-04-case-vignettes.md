# Module 4 — First Client Case: Case Vignette Bank

One vignette is assigned per small group at the start of Week 5. Each is deliberately left open — no single "correct" treatment plan — so that three weeks of case staffing produce real discussion rather than a fill-in-the-blank exercise. Household composition supports the genogram; environmental context supports the ecomap; the presenting concern is intentionally broad enough to require collaborative goal negotiation, not a prescribed fix.

---

## 1. The Sullivan–Reid Family

**Household:** Ms. Odessa Sullivan (62, grandmother) is raising her two grandchildren, Ja'Kobe (14) and Aaliyah (9), since their mother — Ms. Sullivan's daughter, Renee — entered residential substance-use treatment eight months ago. Renee's whereabouts and recovery progress are inconsistent from the children's point of view; contact is sporadic. The children's father is not involved.

**Presenting concern:** The school has contacted Ms. Sullivan twice this term about Ja'Kobe's behavior (class disruptions, one physical altercation); Aaliyah is doing well academically but has become withdrawn at home.

**Environmental context:** Ms. Sullivan is active in her church (choir, weekly attendance) and draws support from a close circle of church "aunties." She receives a fixed pension and some SNAP benefits; the household is financially tight but stable. No formal kinship-care subsidy has been applied for. Ja'Kobe's school has a counselor but Ms. Sullivan hasn't engaged with them yet.

**Strengths:** Strong extended kinship network, consistent housing, grandmother's clear commitment to both children, existing faith community.

**Left open for the group:** Whether/how to involve Renee in planning given her uncertain recovery timeline; whether Ja'Kobe's behavior is grief/instability-related or needs separate behavioral-health assessment; whether to pursue formal kinship-care support.

## 2. The Okafor Family

**Household:** Mr. and Mrs. Okafor emigrated from Nigeria twelve years ago. Their daughter, Chidinma ("Chi," 16), was born in the U.S. Mrs. Okafor's mother lives with them part of the year. An adult son, Emeka (24), lives independently in another state.

**Presenting concern:** Escalating conflict between Chi and her parents over independence (curfew, dating, college choice); Chi has become disengaged at school and increasingly distant from her grandmother, with whom she was previously close.

**Environmental context:** The family attends a Nigerian cultural association's monthly gatherings, which Chi has stopped attending. Mr. Okafor works long hours in logistics; Mrs. Okafor works part-time and manages most of the household and eldercare. Extended family remains in Nigeria; contact is frequent but geographically distant. Chi's school has no staff who share her cultural background.

**Strengths:** Stable household income and housing, extended family engagement (though currently strained), grandmother's continued presence in the home, Chi's academic history is otherwise strong.

**Left open for the group:** How to frame the conflict — generational/acculturation gap vs. typical adolescent independence-seeking vs. both; whether the grandmother should be engaged as a bridge or is part of the tension; how much weight the cultural association's estrangement should carry in assessment.

## 3. The Washington Family

**Household:** Mr. Terrence Washington (41) returned home eight months ago after a five-year incarceration. He has remarried; his wife, Denise (38), has two children from a previous relationship — Marcus (12) and Kayla (10) — who are getting to know their stepfather. Terrence's biological daughter from a prior relationship, Simone (15), visits every other weekend.

**Presenting concern:** Marcus and Kayla have mixed reactions to Terrence's presence in the home — Kayla is warming up, Marcus is withdrawn and resistant. Terrence is struggling to find stable employment due to his record, which is straining the household financially and, he reports, his own sense of purpose. Simone has expressed to Denise that she feels caught between two households.

**Environmental context:** Denise works full-time; Terrence has had two job offers rescinded after background checks. The family attends a reentry support group Terrence found through a community organization. Marcus's school is aware of the family transition but has not been formally looped in.

**Strengths:** Terrence's active engagement with a reentry support group, Denise's stability and employment, no substance-use concerns reported, the couple's explicit commitment to blended-family integration.

**Left open for the group:** How to sequence goals across three interlocking needs (employment barriers, stepfamily integration, Simone's sense of belonging across two households) without treating them as separate cases; whether/how the reentry support group should be treated as a formal part of the plan.

## 4. Ms. Bettye Coleman and Family

**Household:** Ms. Bettye Coleman (78) lives alone in the home she's owned for 40 years. Her three adult children — Patricia (52), Michael (49), and Deborah (46) — live within an hour's drive and disagree about her care: Patricia believes it's time for assisted living, Michael believes she's managing fine, Deborah is undecided and feels caught in the middle.

**Presenting concern:** Ms. Coleman had a fall three months ago (no major injury) and has since had two instances of leaving the stove on. She is firm that she wants to remain in her home. A referral came from her primary care physician's office.

**Environmental context:** Ms. Coleman is a longtime member of her church and still drives to services independently, though her children have noticed she's stopped driving at night. She has a neighbor who checks in informally. No home health services are currently in place; the family has not explored what's available or its cost.

**Strengths:** Strong cognitive baseline (no dementia indicators reported), clear personal preferences she can articulate, informal neighbor support, adult children who — despite disagreement — are all actively involved.

**Left open for the group:** How to center Ms. Coleman's own stated preference within a family system that disagrees; what "safety" versus "autonomy" means in this specific case rather than in the abstract; what resources (formal or informal) could change the family's either/or framing.

## 5. The Torres–Bell Family

**Household:** Ms. Angela Torres-Bell (33) is raising two children alone: Marcus (11), who has an Individualized Education Program (IEP) for a behavioral health need, and Nia (6). The children's fathers are not consistently involved. The family has been staying with Ms. Torres-Bell's sister for four months after losing their apartment.

**Presenting concern:** Ms. Torres-Bell works two part-time jobs and has had difficulty attending Marcus's IEP meetings and follow-up behavioral-health appointments. The living arrangement with her sister is described by both as "temporary but stretching thin."

**Environmental context:** Marcus's school has an active IEP team but has flagged inconsistent attendance at review meetings. The family has not yet connected with housing assistance programs. Ms. Torres-Bell describes her sister as her primary support but notes rising tension in the shared household.

**Strengths:** Ms. Torres-Bell's consistent employment (despite the toll), an existing IEP already in place for Marcus (not starting from zero), an available — if strained — kinship housing resource.

**Left open for the group:** Whether the priority sequence should be housing stability first (as a precondition for everything else) or behavioral-health/school engagement first; how to work with, not around, the sister's household as a temporary but real support system.

## 6. Jamal Carter

**Household:** Jamal Carter (19) aged out of foster care at 18 after seven years in the system, including four different placements. He has minimal contact with his biological family (a mother with whom contact is estranged; a maternal grandmother he sees rarely). He currently lives in a transitional living program with a curfew and case-management check-ins.

**Presenting concern:** Jamal's transitional housing program ends in five months. He has an inconsistent work history (two retail jobs, both ended within a few months) and has expressed to his case manager both a desire for independence and anxiety about "having nobody" once the program ends.

**Environmental context:** Jamal has one former foster placement (a foster parent he still calls occasionally) who could potentially be a support, though he's ambivalent about reaching out. He is not currently connected to mental-health services despite the transitional program recommending it twice. He has expressed interest in a trade certification program but hasn't enrolled.

**Strengths:** Jamal's own stated self-awareness about his situation, an existing (if underused) potential support connection, a concrete stated interest (trade certification) to build a goal around.

**Left open for the group:** How to build a treatment plan around a client whose formal "family" system is nearly absent — what does a genogram and ecomap even center on when kinship ties are this thin; how directive versus client-led the planning should be given the housing timeline pressure.
