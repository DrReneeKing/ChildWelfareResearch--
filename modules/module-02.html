Understanding the Child Welfare System

Weeks 3–4 Wed 8/28/26 & Fri 9/4/26 Data Science Student Influencer Cohort

Overview

With orientation and mandated reporting established, this module builds students' foundational understanding of the child welfare system itself: its historical roots, how maltreatment is defined and categorized, and the multisystem network of agencies, courts, and services that respond to it. This is also where the first named data-science moment of the semester shows up — the Data Science Student Influencer Cohort activity — a lightweight, low-stakes touchpoint ahead of DSSWE Module 1 proper (Week 6–7).

Learning Outcome

Discuss maltreatment of children from a historical perspective; explain the multisystem approach to child welfare services, including the roles of the courts, agencies, and community organizations that intersect in a case.

Weekly Breakdown

Week 3 (8/28/26) — Child Maltreatment & History ▸
  • Defining child maltreatment: the four recognized types (physical abuse, neglect, sexual abuse, psychological maltreatment) and how they're distinguished in practice
  • Historical overview of child welfare policy in the U.S. — from early charitable/institutional models to the modern child protection system
  • How historical policy choices still shape current practice, caseloads, and racial disparities in the system
  • DS Touchpoint (Week 3): a first-look tour of the RStudio interface — console, environment, and files panes, by name only, no coding yet. See The Four RStudio Panes in the reference guide for the same tour with color-coded labels. Maps to the Pre/Post-Survey's RStudio-structure knowledge-check item.
Week 4 (9/4/26) — Multisystem Approach to Child Welfare Services ▸
  • The multisystem network: child protective services, family/juvenile courts, schools, healthcare, law enforcement, and community-based organizations
  • How a single case moves across these systems, and where communication breakdowns most often occur
  • Court oversight in action — the Kenny A. v. Perdue consent decree: in 2002, Children's Rights filed a federal class-action lawsuit on behalf of children in foster care in Fulton and DeKalb Counties; the 2005 consent decree requires Georgia to achieve and sustain 31 specific outcome measures — including caseworker caseload limits, sibling co-placement, timely health screenings for children entering care, and reduced reliance on group/congregate placements — monitored by independent accountability agents who issue public compliance reports every six months. This is the multisystem network's court-oversight layer made concrete, and it's the specific consent decree this course keeps returning to (see Module 06, and the Case Study).
  • Data Science Student Influencer Cohort: an early, informal activity introducing students to the idea that data connects these systems — no coding required yet, just framing (see Data Science Tie-In below)

Data Science Tie-In

This module carries the first named data-science touchpoint of the semester, though still deliberately low-stakes:

  • Data Science Student Influencer Cohort (Week 4): frames the multisystem content itself as a data problem — different agencies hold different pieces of a child's record, and connecting them is fundamentally a data-integration challenge. This is discussion/reflection, not a coding activity.
  • What's coming: the DSSWE Pre-Survey lands the following week (9/11/26), and DSSWE Module 1 (Introduction to Data Science) begins for real in the Week 6–7 asynchronous window. Use this module to keep reinforcing that no prior coding experience is needed and that skills build incrementally from here.
  • A real example, in passing: the Kenny A. consent decree's 31 outcome measures are themselves a data-accountability system — a court requiring a state agency to prove compliance with quantified, tracked measures every six months. Worth a one-sentence callout when introducing it: this is what "holding a system accountable with data" looks like outside a classroom.
